# 11 · Choosing and paying a GM

Who the GM is decides how far each business goes. Good GMs make a business fly; weak ones
cap it, whatever the agents do.

## Who
- Already works there, senior, knows every client, trusted by the staff. Often the person the
  best clients are loyal to (the 15-year senior bookkeeper, not the newest manager).
- Wants to run it, not just keep doing the work. Ask them what they would change first; the
  best answer is usually "nothing yet, I want to see the numbers".
- Comfortable being the approver. The GM is the accountability layer for every client message.

## How to check
- Diligence interview (runbook 08) plus the seller's honest read.
- The 30-day shadow period is a real trial: are they logging corrections, clearing the queue,
  and raising problems early?

## The deal (confirm structure and tax treatment with counsel and a CPA)
- **Base:** a real raise on day one. They are taking on more.
- **Real upside:** equity in the operating company, for example a 5–15% profits interest in an
  LLC (or phantom equity if simpler), vesting over 4 years with a 1-year cliff, with
  accelerated vesting on a sale.
- **Annual bonus on all five Monday numbers**, not margin alone: margin, minutes per job, the
  share of drafts needing fixes, client retention, and key people staying. Paying on margin
  alone rewards losing clients and burning out staff.
- **Decision rights in writing:** hiring, client exceptions, pricing within a band, what needs you.
- **Their name on the work:** client messages come from the GM (the demo's "— Dana" rule).

## Warning signs
- Wants to change everything in month one.
- Hides or skips corrections ("easier to just fix it myself" without logging).
- Won't approve anything (becomes the bottleneck) or approves everything unread.
- Staff pulse drops after the change of ownership.

## Your side of the bargain
Back office, agents, rules, dashboards, capital, and a weekly call where you ask "how can I
help?" and then actually help.
